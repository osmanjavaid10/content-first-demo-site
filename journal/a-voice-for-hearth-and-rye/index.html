A voice for Hearth & Rye

How a village bakery learned to sound like itself, from the shop window to the flour sacks.

By Nora Alder2 September 20261 min read

When Tomas Reyes first wrote to me, his bakery had a problem most businesses would envy. The bread was selling out by nine. The queue ran past the post office. But the website, the bags, and the sign in the window all sounded like three different companies, and none of them sounded like Tomas.

"I talk about bread all day," he told me. "Then I sit down to write about it and I turn into a brochure."

Listening first

I spent three mornings in the bakery, starting at four. I did not write a word. I listened to how Tomas spoke to his bakers, to the delivery driver, to the woman who buys a half loaf every day and has opinions about the crust. He was funny, exact, and never once used the word artisan.

That sentence, said over his shoulder while shaping a loaf, became the first line of the website. Most of the voice was already there. It only needed writing down.

Three rules, one page

The finished voice guide fits on a single sheet pinned beside the ovens. Say what is in it. Say how long it took. Never tell anyone how to feel about it. Everything else, from the menu boards to the replies on social media, follows from those three.

Six months on, the queue is no shorter, but the bakery finally sounds like the man who runs it. The flour sacks now read, in full: Stoneground wheat. Milled twelve miles away. Do not rush.
