What the shelves remember

A second-hand bookshop is closing after forty-one years. I spent its last week behind the counter, listening.

By Nora Alder14 October 20263 min read

The bell above the door at Northlight Books has a crack in it, so it does not ring so much as cough. Margaret Hale has been meaning to replace it since 1987. "It tells me someone's come in," she says. "I never needed it to be pretty about it."

She opened the shop at twenty-six with a van full of books from a house clearance and a loan from an uncle who thought she was mad. This month, at sixty-seven, she will lock the door for the last time. Nobody is forcing her out. The rent is fair and the roof is sound. She is simply ready, she says, to read some of them.

A week at the counter

I asked if I could spend the final week working beside her, and she agreed on the condition that I learned the system. The system is this: fiction runs along the left wall by author, history along the right by century, and everything else lives where Margaret feels it belongs. Cookery is next to travel, because both are about wanting to be somewhere else. Poetry has the back room to itself, with the only comfortable chair.

By Tuesday I could find most things. By Thursday I understood that finding things was never the point. People do not come to a shop like this to collect a book they already know they want. They come to be surprised by one they did not.

The regulars arrive in a kind of tide. Retired teachers at ten. Students after lunch, counting coins. A man called Edwin who has bought one green Penguin crime novel every Friday for thirty years and has, by Margaret's reckoning, now read some of them four times. She keeps a short stack aside for him under the till. When I ask what he will do in November, she is quiet for a moment, and then says she has put his name on a box.

What gets left inside

Second-hand books carry cargo. In one week I found a pressed fern, a bus ticket to Carlisle dated 1974, a shopping list that read only candles, string, apologise, and a photograph of two women laughing on a pier. Margaret keeps the best of these in a biscuit tin. She has never thrown one away.

"They're not mine to bin," she says. "Somebody stopped reading right there, on that page, and put that in to hold their place. Maybe they meant to come back." She turns the photograph over. Nothing is written on it. "I like to think they just had somewhere better to be."

The last Saturday

On the final day the shop is fuller than it has been in years. People who have not visited since they were children bring their own children. Someone leaves flowers on the counter with no card. A woman buys the comfortable chair from the poetry room for forty pounds and carries it out herself, over her head, like a trophy.

At five o'clock Margaret turns the sign. The bell coughs once more as the last customer leaves. She stands for a while with her hand flat on the fiction shelves, somewhere around the letter M, and then she picks up her coat.

The books are going to a dealer in Hexham, apart from one box marked Edwin. The biscuit tin is going home with her. The bell, cracked as it is, she has given to me. It is on my desk as I write this. It still does not ring. I would not change it for anything.
